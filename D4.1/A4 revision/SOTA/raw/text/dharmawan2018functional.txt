2018 3rd International Conference on Advanced Robotics and Mechatronics (ICARM)

Maximizing Robot Manipulator’s Functional Redundancy via Sequential Informed Optimization
Audelia G. Dharmawan, Suhasini Padmanathan, Yi Xiong, Inigo F. Ituarte, Shaohui Foong, and Gim Song Soh

Abstract— This paper proposes Sequential Informed Optimization (SIO) method, a computationally-efﬁcient approach for a globally-optimal task-constrained motion planning. It builds upon the idea of using the knowledge of the preceding robot’s task and globally-optimum conﬁguration as the intellectual initial guess to guide and plan for the subsequent robot’s motion. The proposed approach can be used with any standard constrained nonlinear optimization method. The abilities of the method to plan the globally-optimum motion for a functionally-redundant manipulator, i.e. robot executing ‘soft’ task constraints, are demonstrated in the numerical simulations.
I. INTRODUCTION Additive manufacturing (AM) is a technology that has been recently gaining attention due to its potential to enable innovation in the design process, reduced manufacturing cost, and shorter time to market [1]. Typically the setup consists of a motion system, a head source, and a feedstock to built a part layer by layer through process-speciﬁc motion control. For large-scale metal manufacturing, the combination of an electric arc as the head source, wire as the feedstock, and robotic system for AM purpose (referred to as WAAM [2], as shown in Fig. 1) is rather common and has been investigated since the 1990s.
Fig. 1. Illustration of a robotic WAAM (Wire Arc Additive Manufacturing) printing large-scale thin-walled metallic structures. The end-effector rotation about the approach direction (indicated by the arrow) is irrelevant to the completion of the task, creating a functional redundancy.

The main challenge faced by this approach is the effect of residual stress as introduced by the heat input of the arc source, leading to part distortion once it is unclamped. To overcome this, it requires the consideration of a variety of process-related kinematic constraints into its motion planner. An advantage of using a 6-DOF (degree of freedom) robot manipulator for WAAM is its functional redundancy, as its end-effector rotation about the approach direction is irrelevant to the completion of the task. A robot manipulator with m DOF is said to be functionally redundant when only a number of r components in its operational space are of concern for the speciﬁc task, with r < m [3]. The extra DOF thus creates an inﬁnite number of robot conﬁgurations which can accomplish the task. The redundant DOF can be exploited in the robot motion planning to optimize a desired performance criterion by selecting the robot conﬁguration.
In the past, Jacobian pseudo-inverse method has been used to resolve redundancy particularly in robotic welding application [4]. However, this method is considered a local optimization where the resulting output may not be the global optimum depending on the initial robot conﬁguration. Another approach is using genetic algorithm (GA) [5], which is capable of producing globally-optimum solutions. However, GA suffers from long computational time and thus has limited usage for online implementation. Other alternative approach is through the use of constrained optimization [6]. Similar to the Jacobian pseudo-inverse approach, this approach is also very prone to falling into local minima depending on the initial guess.
The common issue in typical optimization approaches is the difﬁculty in choosing the initial guess that leads to the globally-optimum solution. One way is to solve the optimization problem using multiple initial guesses. However, this has a drawback of longer computational time and does not guarantee a global solution. Thus, a compromise must be made between obtaining the globally-optimum solution and the computational cost. Another approach is through using fewer ‘intellectual’ guesses. For example in [7], a heuristic approach is used by solving a simpliﬁed problem which helps to generate more useful initial guesses for the more complex ﬁnal problem. This is fundamentally a similar idea as numerical continuation method [8].
One issue in the above-mentioned solutions is that it is not easy to construct and solve the simpler problem. Thus in [9], we proposed the sequential expanded lagrangian homotopy (SELH) method that makes use of the knowledge of the preceding task and globally-optimum robot conﬁguration as the simpler problem to guide and solve the optimal robot

II. SEQUENTIAL INFORMED OPTIMIZATION In this section, we describe the proposed idea of Sequential Informed Optimization (SIO) for a computationally-efﬁcient globally-optimal task-contrained motion planning. Informed optimization here means that instead of blindly using random values as initial guesses, the information of the preceding optimal robot’s conﬁguration is utilized to determine its subsequent optimal conﬁguration. Our rationale is that if the previous robot’s conﬁguration is already globally optimal, using it as the initial guess will more likely lead to the globally-optimum solution for the next task. One limitation for this is that the starting robot conﬁguration which fulﬁls the ﬁrst task in the task sequence needs to be solved for global optimality. Nevertheless, this can be obtained ofﬂine ﬁrst even through simple exhaustive search, which we term here as ‘global initalization’ step. Once this is done, the determination of the of the next optimal conﬁguration can be obtained, leading to a computationally-efﬁcient globallyoptimal task-constrained motion planner.

IV. NUMERICAL SIMULATIONS
In this section, numerical simulations are performed to demonstrate the performance and capability of the proposed method. We examine the approach on printing a layer of two different print tasks (illustrated by the two printed structures in Fig. 1), a small square thin-walled print job and a large rectangular thin-walled print job which pushes the robot to its reach limit. The small square thin-wall measures 50 x 50 cm2 while the large rectangular thin-wall measures 1.6 x 0.9 m2. The continuous print task is discretized at 5-cm resolution for the numerical simulations. The motion planning optimization problem as in Eq. 9 is solved using Sequential Quadratic Programming (SQP). The ground truth solutions are obtained through exhaustive search to validate the results obtained by SIO. In terms of the resulting manipulability measure, the value is also compared with when the γ rotation of the endeffector is ﬁxed instead of optimized.
Fig. 4 shows the results for the small print task while Fig. 5 shows the results for the large print task. The ground truth results from exhaustive search intermittently swap between robot’s ‘elbow right’ and ‘elbow left’ solutions as they yield comparable manipulability measure values. Owing to the sequential intellectual intial guess which guides the motion planning for the subsequent task, the proposed SIO approach is able to smoothen the results to obtain the closest comparable globally-optimum solutions.
As seen from the results, the SIO approach is able to obtain the correct globally-optimum solutions as compared to the gound truth results. The seemingly erroneous γ angle results at the end of the large print job are due to the 360◦ offset of the angle values; they are indeed giving the correct results. The average computational time to produce the subsequent optimum conﬁguration for each new task point is around 20 ms, i.e. for a functionally-redundant robot, the SIO approach is capable of providing an online motion planning with a 50 Hz feedback loop frequency. V. CONCLUSIONS
An approach to efﬁciently compute the globally-optimum solutions to the problem of task-constrained motion planning and redundancy resolution is explored in this paper. The proposed Sequential Informed Optimization (SIO) approach takes advantage of the knowledge of the preceding robot’s globally-optimal conﬁguration as the intellectual initial guess to the subsequent motion planning optimization problem in the task sequence. In this way, the globally-optimum solutions can be efﬁciently searched without the need of randomly trying countless initial guesses. The approach can be applied to any constrained nonlinear optimization method. Numerical simulations demonstrate the viability of the approach in obtaining the globally-optimum solutions, with the ability of providing a real-time feedback loop at 50 Hz due to its quick computational time.
