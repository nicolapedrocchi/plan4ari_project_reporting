An effective path planning approach for robot welding considering redundant kinematics

ABSTRACT
Robot welding with redundant kinematics demands precise coordination of the welding path and redundant axis motion for stable operation. This paper introduces an effective G3 continuous path planning approach for a 6DOF friction stir welding robot, integrating redundant axis motion to enhance welding stability and quality. The method encompasses a two-pronged strategy: a five-axis path corner smoothing technique and a motion synchronization strategy. Initially, a robust toolpath preprocessing algorithm is presented to mitigate curvature extremities of the inserted smoothing curves within predefined error limits. Subsequently, leveraging G3 continuity criteria, a B-spline-based smoothing method is advanced for refining tool position and orientation paths, with an analytical determination of spline control points facilitated by an explicit smoothing error expression. An effective motion synchronization technique is then proposed, which formulates the tool position, tool orientation, and redundant axis path as explicit functions of the position path arc length. The effectiveness of the proposed method is demonstrated through simulations and experiments on a robot welding platform, with the integration of a jerk-continuous feedrate profile for smooth motion execution. The findings indicate a significant improvement in robotic welding quality by integrating the proposed path planning method with existing workpiece posture optimization techniques.

1. Introduction
Friction stir welding (FSW) is a cutting-edge welding technique that employs a high-speed rotating tool plunged into the workpiece, generating frictional heat to soften the materials and form a solid-state weld joint [1]. Similar to milling machining [2–5], FSW has garnered widespread application in sectors such as aerospace, rail transit, shipbuilding, and automotive manufacturing due to its efficiency and superior welding quality [6]. The FSW process equipment endorsed by the industry is categorized into three types: machine tool-based, articulated robot-based, and parallel robot-based equipment, each with its unique advantages. Importantly, the versatility to produce various products on a single system is pivotal for manufacturing success, as noted in Ref. [7]. Especially noteworthy is the FSW equipment that integrates parallel/hybrid robots with a dedicated welding spindle. This setup offers superior accuracy, rigidity, workspace, and flexibility,

making it ideal for complex spatial curve welding tasks on medium-thick plates [8]. Although the rigidity of parallel/hybrid robotic systems is 3–4 times greater than that of articulated robots, elastic deformation due to welding forces can still compromise the welding accuracy. To counteract this, a movable worktable with one degree of freedom (DOF) has been introduced [9]. Considering the decoupling nature of the welding robot and the worktable motion, the precise synchronization of the tool-tip, the tool axis, and the redundant axis motion, under the premise of known redundant axis variables, remains challenging.
Trajectory planning is essential for the precision and efficiency of welding operations on the foundation of superior kinematic and dynamic characteristics of the equipment. Traditionally, the CNC programming of welding paths follows the ISO 6983 standard (i.e., G-code), which specifies both the tool position path (TPP) and tool orientation path (TOP) [10]. However, the potential discontinuities in tangency and curvature at junction points of the G-code path may disrupt the

Hence, the generation of a higher-order continuous welding path through smoothing techniques is essential [11], which can be performed along the geometric toolpath (G continuity) and the trajectory profile (C continuity) [12]. Two predominant toolpath smoothing approaches have emerged: global smoothing and corner smoothing. Global smoothing, particularly adept for intricate machining with shorter path segments, focuses on path reconstruction through curve fitting or interpolation techniques [13, 14].
Corner smoothing, which utilizes parametric curves to replace the corners of original path segments, offers the advantage of controllable smoothing errors, making it suitable for scenarios with longer path segments [15]. Extensive research indicates that the essence of corner smoothing lies in leveraging geometric path information and preset smoothing error tolerances to determine the smoothing curve control points. Commonly used smoothing curves include NURBS [16], B-splines [17], Bezier curves [18], PH curves [19], polynomial curves [20], and clothoid curves [21], each presenting distinct advantages in toolpath representation, smoothing error control, and parameter synchronization. In five-axis welding, the TPP and TOP are customarily smoothed within independent coordinate frameworks. Specifically, the TPP smoothing is primarily carried out in the Cartesian space, while the TOP smoothing usually occurs in the Euler angle space or on the Gaussian sphere space. Some typical methods have been summarized as follows.
(1) In terms of TPP smoothing, existing techniques have achieved G2 [22], G3 [23], and G4 [24] continuity for linear paths. For position paths incorporating arc segments, existing methods, utilizing tools such as B-splines or clothoid curves, have achieved G2 [25] and G3 [26] continuity.
(2) In terms of TOP smoothing, methods bifurcate into indirect smoothing [12,27] and direct smoothing [15,28]. Indirect smoothing initiates by delineating an equivalent path to symbolize the TOP, followed by processing akin to the TOP smoothing. For example, Sun [12] employs a non-coincident point with the tool-tip point on the tool axis direction as the equivalent path; Yang [27] regards the Euler angle path as the equivalent path. Direct smoothing commences by constructing an intermediary curve that fulfills the geometric continuity and smoothing error constraints, then projects this curve onto the Gaussian sphere to ensure the unity norm criteria of the tool axis vector. Typical of such work, Liu [28] analytically formulates control points of the quintic B-spline smoothing curve to achieve G3 continuity and then projects the resulting B-spline onto the Gaussian sphere surface.
In multi-axis machining, the independent smoothing of the TPP and the TOP may lead to abrupt variations in the tool axis angular velocity at junction points [29]. Thus, synchronization of the TPP and TOP has become critical to ensure the smooth motion of the welding equipment [27,28]. Synchronization strategies are broadly categorized into coupling methods [15] and decoupling methods [30]. Coupling methods fine-tune the control points of the orientation smoothing spline to achieve high-order geometric continuity of the TOP and parameter synchronization simultaneously. Typical of such work, Altintas [15] employs a 7th-degree B-spline for the TOP smoothing and utilizes the Newton-Raphson method to determine its control points, thereby ensuring third-order derivative continuity of the TOP to the tool-tip displacement. Decoupling methods are subdivided into the adjustment approach [27,28] and the parameter mapping approach [10,29]. The adjustment method, following independent smoothing of the TPP and TOP, adjusts the length (or angle) of the remaining path segments or reparameterizes them for synchronization. For example, Yang [27] adjusts the remaining path segment lengths for the five-axis linear toolpath, and Liu [28] reparameterizes the remaining path segments into a

specially designed 7th-degree B-spline. The parameter mapping technique employs specialized functions to depict the mathematical relationship between orientation and position parameters. In this regard, Beudaert [30] describes the toolpath with two B-splines and introduces a third to represent the mathematical relationship between the path parameters; Yuen [10] establishes an analytical expression relating the TOP parameter to the TPP parameter through polynomial fitting; Zhang [29] generates a C3 continuous synchronization curve by interpolating the arc-length pairs of the TPP and TOP at junction points.
In 6-DOF machining equipment research, extraneous rotational or translational freedoms do not inherently alter the tool posture expressed in the workpiece coordinate system (WCS). However, they significantly influence the joint movements of the welding equipment through inverse kinematics. This distinction underscores that 6-DOF robot operations demand a more intricate path-planning approach than 5-DOF machinery, incorporating the need for redundant axis path smoothing and synchronization. Recently, Yang [31] developed a novel PH-based path corner smoothing technique for a 6R robot, with the TPP, TOP, and redundant axis effectively synchronized to the arc length parameter of the TPP. In Yang’s method, modifications to the redundant axis commands are isolated and do not interfere with the other joint variables of the 6R robot. In our previous work about FSW [32], an effective solution for the redundant axis position has been developed to improve the welding quality. For more about trajectory planning based on stiffness, please see Ref. [33]. However, the challenge of achieving synchronized motion for the TPP, the TOP, and the redundant axis remains an area ripe for exploration. To sum up, there is substantial potential for refining existing path smoothing and synchronization methods for 6-DOF equipment with redundant kinematics, i.e.
(1) Existing methods primarily concentrate on limiting the extrema of smoothing errors, often neglecting their distributional characteristics, making it challenging for curvature optimization of the inserted smoothing curves.
(2) In scenarios characterized by dense cutter location (CL) point distributions, existing methods tend to shorten the length of smoothing path segments to prevent curve overlap, which can escalate curvature extrema and reduce motion efficiency. Prioritizing high-order geometric continuity over parametric continuity during the independent smoothing of the TPP and TOP can improve the curvature characteristics of smoothing curves.
(3) Existing parameter synchronization methods often employ shared curve parameters for both position and orientation splines. This strategy, aimed at achieving high-order parameter continuity at junction points, might excessively restrict the smoothing path length and cause potential oscillations in the tool axis and joint trajectories near these points.
This paper develops an effective path-planning method for a 6-DOF robot welding equipment with redundant kinematics. The main innovations are summarized as follows.
(1) An effective path smoothing method is developed for the 6-DOF welding equipment featuring a redundant axis, ensuring synchronized motion across tool position, orientation, and redundant axis paths.
(2) A G3-continuous corner smoothing method considering the error constraints is proposed, enabling the control points for both position and orientation smoothing splines to be analytically constructed.
(3) A B-spline-based synchronization method by interpolating the arc-length pairs is presented, which can effectively improve the smoothness of tool axis motion.
(4) A method leveraging quintic polynomial interpolation is proposed to synchronize motion between the redundant axis and the

In this section, a smoother welding path that exhibits a diminished curvature extreme is achieved through the integration of corner smoothing and path preprocessing techniques. 6. Conclusions
This paper presents a G3 continuous path-planning method for a robot welding system with redundant kinematics, addressing both path corner smoothing and motion synchronization. The conclusions are as follows.
(1) A path preprocessing algorithm is developed to optimize the curvature characteristics of the smoothing B-splines at path junctions while adhering to error constraints. Simulation results of an S-shaped path show that the algorithm outperforms existing smoothing methods in error control, and significantly reduces the curvature extremum by 43 %.
(2) A B-spline-based corner smoothing and synchronization strategy for the five-axis welding path is introduced. Motion synchronization of the TOP and TPP is achieved by solving a B-spline-based optimization problem, targeting the minimization of squared curvature derivatives. Simulation results of a blade path demonstrate a 31 % reduction in the TPP curvature extremum and a 41 % reduction in that of the TOP compared to the existing method. Additionally, the arc lengths of the TOP and TPP met monotonic increasing and C3 continuity constraints, with an 84 % decrease in tool axis angular acceleration and over a 95 % reduction in angular jerk.
(3) A polynomial-based motion synchronization strategy for the redundant axis is proposed to ensure the continuity of its third derivative versus the TPP arc length. Simulation results of a spatial welding path confirm the smoothness of the resultant motion trajectories.
(4) Experiments conducted on the 6-DOF robot welding platform, juxtaposed against a static redundant axis setup, demonstrate that integrating existing workpiece posture optimization technology with the proposed path-planning method significantly improves welding quality.
(5) The proposed method is not only applicable to welding equipment with redundant axes but also shows potential for extension to other multi-axis machining equipment.
