Innovation in robot welding trajectory control: Online synchronous planning of welding paths and torch postures for structures with unknown spatial geometries
Hao Wang a, Xiujun Wang b, Chunguo Liu a, Chao Chen a, Xiaohui Zhao a,*
a Key Laboratory of Automobile Materials, School of Materials Science and Engineering, Jilin University, Changchun 130025, China b Inner Mongolia Institute of Special Equipment Inspection and Research, Chifeng 024000, China

ARTICLE INFO
Keywords: Robot GMAW Welding path planning Torch posture planning Line laser vision

ABSTRACT
To address the limitations of existing welding trajectory planning schemes, which predominantly focus on welding paths while neglecting collaboration of torch postures and overly rely on prior models or idealized assumptions, an innovative solution for online synchronous planning of welding paths and torch postures for structures with unknown spatial geometries was developed. The GMAW system assisted by line laser vision was independently designed and implemented. The camera calibration leveraging the circular tangential gradient orthogonal method (CTGOM) and adaptive particle swarm optimization (APSO) was formulated. Optical plane calibration and hand-eye calibration were then sequentially performed, achieving an average absolute error of 0.1111 mm in the robot’s perception of spatial targets. The cascaded 3D inverse modeling framework for spatial joints was established, successively employing YOLOv11, context information-based iterative centerline extraction algorithm (CIICEA), Pauta criterion and K-means-based quasi-uniform cubic B-spline fitting algorithm (K-QCBFA). The approach for online planning of robot welding trajectories based on point cloud geometric differentiation was proposed. By constructing local differential geometries from groove cross-sectional planes and base metal tangent planes, the full six-degree-of-freedom welding trajectories (XB, YB, ZB, RX, RY , RZ) for unknown spatial geometries were planned synchronously online. The root mean square error (RMSE) for online planning of welding paths in butt and fillet joints was 0.0829 mm and 0.1334 mm, respectively, while that for torch postures was 0.7782◦ and 0.8064◦, respectively. The proposed welding trajectory planning scheme demonstrates exceptional adaptability to complex spatial geometries, multi-layer multi-pass welding, and diverse joint configurations.

1. Introduction
In the evolution from teaching-playback welding to autonomous welding, mechanical, arc, infrared, ultrasonic, electromagnetic, and optical sensors have been extensively integrated into welding systems to endow welding robots or specialized machines with the capability to dynamically perceive spatial targets [1,2]. Although these sensors have significantly improved welding intelligence in key areas such as workpiece positioning [3,4], arc starting and extinguishing point detection [5–7], joint type identification [8,9], welding path fitting [10,11], and welding path tracking [12–15], most existing approaches remain confined to the path control for special spatial structures without addressing the autonomous planning and adjustment of torch postures.

This limitation poses a critical barrier to achieving full six-degree-of-freedom trajectory control required for arbitrary 3D complex structures. In particular, for engineering structural components such as centrifuges, excavators, hydrogenation reactors, and ships, conventional path-level approaches with fixed torch postures are increasingly inadequate to meet the urgent demand for efficient and high-precision autonomous welding.
In intelligent robot welding, the welding trajectory (XB, YB, ZB, RX, RY, RZ) represents a full six-degree-of-freedom combination of the welding path and torch posture. The welding path (XB, YB, ZB) determines the spatial curve of the wire tip movement, ensuring continuous filler metal deposition while enhancing welding efficiency [16]. The torch posture (RX, RY, RZ) directly affects arc stability, molten pool

Only through the online collaborative planning and control of both the welding path and torch posture can the requirements for highly adaptive and reliable intelligent welding be fully satisfied. In complex and dynamic welding environments, robustly achieving these objectives remains a core challenge requiring urgent breakthroughs [18].
Currently, intelligent robot welding commonly employs the static preset mode of torch posture, relying solely on the welding path as the fundamental basis for trajectory control. Robot teaching programming guides the welding torch through the teach pendant, recording and storing the coordinates of key trajectory points prior to welding for subsequent welding execution [19,20]. However, the determination of the welding path and torch posture during this process relies heavily on manual experience and operational precision. By capturing the surface of the workpiece to be welded using monocular or binocular vision and employing image processing techniques, welding paths can be effectively extracted prior to welding [4,21]. Li et al. [22,23] utilized the monocular camera to capture the planar seam region, subsequently acquiring the coordinates of each point along the welding path through image processing. Dinham et al. [24,25] captured the profile of planar seams using binocular stereo vision, extracted key feature points to perform stereo matching, and thereby determined spatial welding paths. However, this path recognition approach is susceptible to interference from workpiece reflections, residual spatter, and illumination variations, and its application in online planning for spatial seam trajectories is limited by the lack of accurate depth information. By continuously scanning the joint area using line laser vision, the high-density 3D point cloud is acquired to reconstruct the complete geometric morphology of the joint, thereby enabling high-precision identification of the welding path prior to welding [26,27]. Liu et al. [28] planned multi-layer and multi-pass welding paths for intersection line joints based on the morphological point cloud acquired by the line laser sensor and utilized the preset sensor scanning orientation as the reference for the torch posture throughout the welding process. Zhang et al. [29] converted the 3D point cloud of the joint, acquired by the line laser sensor, into the 2D grayscale image for feature extraction, and reconstructed the spatial welding path using the cubic spline function. Although line laser vision enables the acquisition of global geometric morphologies of joints to be welded before welding, studies [28,29] did not leverage the geometric information for autonomous planning of the torch posture. Real-time detection of welding path deviations using active or passive vision, combined with dynamic correction, enables high-precision welding during the process [30]. Shen et al. [31,32] employed active vision, namely line laser sensors, to extract the offset between the groove center and the torch, enabling real-time path correction during the welding process of straight and curved seams. Conversely, Zou et al. [33,34] utilized passive vision, specifically monocular industrial cameras, to detect the deviation between the groove center and the arc center, dynamically adjusting the torch position accordingly. However, such path tracking methods typically focus on detecting and dynamically compensating for the horizontal and vertical deviations of the welding torch, whereas autonomous recognition and control of torch posture are frequently overlooked due to high implementation complexity.
Compared to the static preset mode of torch posture, the dynamic adaptive mode has emerged as a key direction for breakthroughs in intelligent robot welding. However, the online adaptive control of torch posture involves complex challenges such as multivariable coupling and spatial geometric modeling, resulting in significantly higher technical complexity than the online control of the welding path. Zou et al. [35, 36] achieved adaptive adjustment of the torch posture by acquiring the 3D point cloud of the joint and computing the real-time attitude angles of the torch relative to the workpiece. However, this method requires the preset of the initial welding path and torch posture. Should the workpiece geometry be unknown or deviate from the hypothetical model, this

trajectory control approach frequently fails. Jia et al. [37] established the mathematical model of T-type off-axis non-groove intersecting joint, achieving autonomous planning of welding trajectories for intersecting joints by analyzing laser point clouds of the joint surface. However, this method relies heavily on prior geometric knowledge, such as pipe radiuses and offset distances, which limits its adaptability to unknown or varying geometries. Wang et al. [38] employed the line laser sensor to pre-scan spatial seams and extracted seam centerlines through point cloud registration and RANSAC algorithm. Based on this, the six-degree-of-freedom welding trajectory incorporating yaw, pitch, and roll angles was generated. Although this method eliminates reliance on real-time tracking, it configures the three rotational angles as decoupled parameters rather than deriving a holistically coordinated posture from groove geometry through coupled optimization. Furthermore, its practicality is restricted to single-pass butt joints, which restricts scalability to multi-layer, multi-pass or fillet joint configurations.
In summary, research on the static preset mode of torch posture has dominated the field of welding trajectory recognition and control. While some studies have investigated dynamic adaptive schemes for torch posture, these efforts still exhibit the following shortcomings: reliance on preset welding trajectories; failure to achieve synergistic recognition and control of both welding path and torch posture; predominantly focus on planar seams or simple spatial seams with known 3D models. Evidently, existing research struggles to provide reliable adaptive collaborative planning solutions for both welding paths and torch postures for unknown spatial geometries.
Given the strengths of line laser vision, namely non-contact operation, robust interference resistance, and capability for 3D spatial target recognition, this study employs it as the core technical approach and systematically implements the end-to-end workflow spanning morphology perception, trajectory planning, and autonomous welding, aiming to establish a highly robust and precise intelligent robot welding solution for diverse complex spatial geometries. The main contributions of this paper are as follows:
(1) The line laser vision-assisted robot GMAW system was designed, comprising a hardware platform integrating core components such as a vision sensor, an industrial PC and a Yaskawa welding robot, alongside control software for multi-task execution covering joint geometry perception, online welding trajectory planning, and intelligent welding. (2) The traditional camera calibration method has been optimized, subsequently integrated with light plane calibration based on backprojected point clouds and spherical geometry-based hand-eye calibration, thereby enabling the GMAW system to accurately perceive the 3D coordinates of spatial targets. (3) An innovative online planning approach was proposed for the welding trajectory of unknown spatial geometries based on point cloud geometric differentiation, enabling autonomous generation of both the welding path and torch posture. (4) The proposed online welding trajectory planning approach was validated using structures of unknown spatial configurations. Comprehensive experiments, encompassing joint 3D morphological reconstruction, online six-degree-of-freedom trajectory planning, and welding execution, were conducted. The adaptability of the robot GMAW system was thoroughly evaluated in actual industrial scenarios.

2. Robot GMAW system assisted by line laser vision
2.1. Hardware platform
As illustrated in Fig. 1, the hardware platform for intelligent robot welding primarily comprises a line laser vision sensor, a Yaskawa welding robot (AR1440), an arc welder (RD350S), a robot control cabinet (YRC1000), and an industrial PC. The self-developed line laser vision sensor consists of a CMOS camera, a red semiconductor laser, and a narrowband optical filter. The industrial PC, the line laser sensor, the robot control cabinet, and the arc welder are interconnected via TCP/IP. Furthermore, all welding specimens in this study were fabricated from Q235 steel, using a shielding gas mixture of 80% Ar + 20% CO₂. The solid welding wire used was THQ-50C with a diameter of 1.2 mm.
2.2. Control software
As illustrated in Fig. 2, the control software for intelligent robot welding, developed using the Yaskawa SDK, comprises five functional modules: display of original line laser stripe image, visualization of 3D morphology of the joint, welding trajectory presentation, real-time monitoring of robot motion parameters, and welding operation control. The control software employs C++ for its core functions, with the interactive interface developed using Qt 6.8.2. The system operates through the following workflow: scanning the joint using the line laser, reverse-engineering the 3D morphology of the joint, planning the welding path and torch posture online, and finally directing the robot to execute the welding operation.

The online planning of welding paths and torch postures for workpieces with unknown spatial geometries remains a critical challenge in intelligent welding. Current research is largely confined to welding paths, failing to achieve effective collaborative recognition and control of both the welding path and the torch posture. 4.1. 3D morphology reverse modeling for joints
4.1.1. Location and classification for joint ROIs Direct geometric morphological reconstruction from large-field-of-
view laser images corrupted by substantial interference typically suffers from low computational efficiency and limited robustness. To overcome these challenges, YOLOv11 is introduced to efficiently localize and classify regions of interest (ROIs) within laser stripes. YOLOv11 maintains a lightweight architecture while achieving highprecision object detection, real-time segmentation, and posture estimation, thereby significantly broadening its applicability in complex visual tasks [42,43]. As illustrated in Fig. 16, the YOLOv11 architecture comprises four primary components: input, backbone, neck, and head. The input employs multi-scale resizing, affine transformation, and color augmentation to improve model generalization. The backbone extracts multi-scale semantic features through an enhanced C3k2 module, which integrates a cross-stage partial (CSP) structure and dual convolutional pathways, and further incorporates fast spatial pyramid pooling (SPPF) and the novel cross-stage spatial attention (C2PSA) module to strengthen sensitivity to small targets and critical regions. The neck facilitates the fusion of high-level semantics and low-level spatial details through the feature pyramid network (FPN). Through decoupling operations, the head independently outputs class confidence, object scores, and bounding box coordinates. These architectural designs enable YOLOv11 to accurately and efficiently identify joint ROIs under complex background conditions, thereby providing a robust foundation for subsequent high-precision reconstruction.
To ensure that YOLOv11 can accurately localize and classify the joint

ROIs even under extreme imaging conditions such as overexposure and underexposure, laser stripe images of various joint specifications, captured under varying exposure levels, viewing angles, interference patterns, were collected and utilized. Representative original samples are shown in Fig. 17. The dataset comprised 1500 images of butt joints and 1500 images of fillet joints. The ratio of images within the training, validation, and test sets was 7:2:1. Notably, to effectively prevent data leakage, the dataset splitting strategy employed in this study was applied specifically to image sets collected during independent experiments, rather than the entire image pool. This ensures that no image in the test set (Fig. 18) shares the identical background, lighting condition, or specific workpiece instance with any image in the training set (Fig. 17), effectively eliminating frame-to-frame correlation leakage. To further enhance generalization, Mosaic data augmentation was employed online during training to ensure the model learns invariant features rather than memorizing specific samples. The online detection performance of the trained YOLOv11 for arbitrary joint ROIs is illustrated in Fig. 18. Results demonstrate that despite variations in illumination, stripe orientation, and background noise, the YOLOv11 consistently achieves precise localization of joint ROIs and accurate classification of joint types within these regions. As presented in Table 5, YOLOv11 achieves both precision and recall of 1.0, indicating the absence of false positives and false negatives in its predictions. The mAP@0.5 scores for both butt joints and fillet joints reach 0.995, reflecting high accuracy in joint type classification and high precision in ROI localization. Notably, the model requires only 4.7 ms per inference, corresponding to a detection frame rate of up to 213 Hz, which fully satisfies the stringent real-time requirements for joint ROI identification and extraction in practical industrial applications.

Figs. 27–28 illustrate the local geometric derivation principle used to compute one welding path point at a given spatial point cloud sample. The complete continuous welding paths are then generated by performing this calculation sequentially along the scanning direction.
